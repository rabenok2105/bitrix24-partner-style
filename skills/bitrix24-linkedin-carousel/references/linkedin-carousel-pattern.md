# LinkedIn Carousel Pattern

This reference captures the recurring structure observed in the Figma file `Banners`, page node `10614:2268`, combined with the local Bitrix24 Partners design assets.

## Core Layout

- The studied LinkedIn slides are landscape frames at `1200 x 628`.
- Most slides use a left text zone and a right visual anchor.
- The right side usually contains one dominant visual: a cutout person, UI mockup, materials stack, or pricing card.
- The series uses a consistent brand shell: dark blue base, lime highlights, Bitrix24 Partners logo, rounded cards, and small accent symbols.

## Slide Types

Use these as the primary building blocks:

1. `Cover`
2. `Table or comparison`
3. `Checklist or process step`
4. `Closing CTA`
5. `Numbered cards`
6. `Educational split slide`
7. `Light CTA or summary`

### Cover

- Large headline on the left.
- Short supporting copy below.
- Cutout person or product visual on the right.
- Logo present and clearly visible.
- Decorative accent icons or stars are acceptable, but keep them secondary.
- In the 3D-icon variant, prefer one clear hero object only.
- Keep the copy block off the bottom edge; it should feel vertically balanced, not dropped to the footer line.

### Table or comparison

- Use for business examples, feature mapping, niches, or plan comparisons.
- Keep the grid strict and aligned.
- Use 2-3 columns with strong column labels.
- Avoid dense paragraphs; each cell should stay skimmable.

### Checklist or process step

- Good for sales stages, onboarding steps, or practical actions.
- A step label or badge can sit in the top-right corner.
- Use 3-5 bullets with icon markers.
- A bottom takeaway strip works well for the key sales message.

### Numbered cards

- Use for frameworks, lessons, factors, or sequential arguments.
- Typically 2-3 cards in one row.
- Each card has a number badge, short heading, and a compact explanatory paragraph.
- This pattern appears repeatedly in the Figma page for educational and market-analysis content.

### Educational split slide

- Use when one side carries explanatory text and the other side carries one illustrative object or a product/support visual.
- The left side is usually a title plus one or two short text blocks.
- The right side should stay simple and should not compete with the copy.

### Light CTA or summary

- A pale background slide can be used as a cleaner ending after several dark slides.
- Suitable for short recap, "why this matters", or soft conversion messaging.
- Maintain the same typography and logo system so the lighter slide still belongs to the same series.

### Closing CTA

- Return to the cover logic: bold headline, simple next step, human visual.
- Add a bright CTA block or button-like element near the bottom.
- Keep the final slide cleaner than the middle slides.

## System-Level Rules

- One main idea per slide.
- Repeat structural elements across the whole series so it reads as one carousel.
- Use headlines, bullets, and cards instead of long paragraphs.
- Keep the bottom insight strip consistent when the concept benefits from a recurring takeaway.
- Use contrast intentionally: dark slide shell, light content cards, lime emphasis.
- Do not overlap primary 3D elements with each other.
- Prefer one main visual object per slide unless a specific comparison layout requires more.
- If using a visual panel on the right, place the object inside it cleanly with breathing room around the edges.
- Decorative accents may repeat, but they must remain secondary to the content and hero object.

## Content Planning

When the user does not provide a slide-by-slide outline, default to:

1. Hook or promise
2. Context or problem framing
3. Practical examples, segmentation, or comparison
4. Actionable process or recommendation
5. CTA

Expand to 6-7 slides only when the topic truly needs more room.

Observed extended patterns from the Figma page:

1. Hook or promise
2. Breakdown by examples, verticals, or factors
3. Process steps or mistakes to avoid
4. Offer, proof, or implementation path
5. CTA or summary

This page also shows that not every carousel needs the same middle structure. Choose between table, numbered cards, checklists, or split-layout explanation based on the topic.
